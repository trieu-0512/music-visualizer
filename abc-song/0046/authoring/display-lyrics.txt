[Intro]
Morning rail: tiny dew drops. Mid-sky: rows of cloud.
We write what we notice before we guess what comes next.

[Verse 1]
A — Altocumulus! Mid-level cloud patches can form rows or rounded clumps | our notebook starts above the morning rooftops.
B ... B ... Breeze! A light wind moves leaves and brushes skin | felt directly, even without an instrument.
C? Cloud! Tiny water droplets or ice crystals gather in air | shape and height become our first visual notes.
D is Dew. Water condenses onto cool surfaces | morning moisture written in beads, not falling rain.

[Verse 2]
E — Earmuffs! Clothing covers the ears in cold weather | gear belongs to the observer, not the forecast itself.
F ... F ... Fog! A cloud at ground level reduces visibility | record how far we can see instead of walking into poor visibility.
G? Gust! A brief stronger burst of wind interrupts the steadier air | one short change worth noting.
H is Hail. Balls or lumps of ice can fall from strong thunderstorms | we stay sheltered and observe safely.

[Chorus]
Look, feel, record—A-B-C!
Weather watching starts with what is really there.

[Verse 3]
I — Ice! Frozen water forms when conditions are cold enough | a surface clue that can make walking slippery.
J ... J ... Jet Stream! Fast narrow bands of wind high in the atmosphere help steer weather systems | invisible, inferred from meteorological data.
K? Kelvin Thermometer! A temperature instrument can display kelvins | measurement clue, not something the sky produces.
L is Lightning. A powerful electrical discharge flashes between cloud regions or cloud and ground | thunder may follow, so shelter matters.

[Verse 4]
M — Mist! Tiny droplets hang near the ground | usually lighter and less visibility-reducing than dense fog.
N ... N ... Night Sky! Darkness reveals stars where clouds and light pollution allow | final time-of-day frame, not a weather instrument.
O? Overcoat! Warm outer clothing helps in cold conditions | choose it from the weather, do not call it weather.
P is Puddle. Rainwater can collect in a shallow low spot | evidence that precipitation reached the ground.

[Verse 5]
Q — Quiet Sky! Our project phrase for a calm-looking moment | observation language, not a scientific cloud type.
R ... R ... Raincoat! Water-resistant outerwear helps keep a person drier in rain | response to weather, not a forecast signal.
S? Snowflake! Ice crystals grow in clouds and may join into snowflakes | delicate structure changes on the way down.
T is Thermometer. Temperature instrument gives a reading | numbers belong in the notebook beside visual clues.

[Chorus]
Look, feel, record—A-B-C!
Weather watching starts with what is really there.

[Verse 6]
U — Updraft! Rising air carries heat and moisture upward | an invisible motion important inside many clouds and storms.
V ... V ... Vapor! Water vapor is invisible water in gas form | visible cloud droplets are a different phase.
W? Windsock! Its orientation shows wind direction and its extension gives a rough sense of wind strength | a station clue we can see.
At the classroom table, X is Xylophone—our X helper | tuned bars mark recall, never pretending to measure weather.

[Verse 7]
Y — Yellow Sun! The Sun can appear yellow from Earth because the atmosphere filters and scatters light | the star itself is not simply a yellow weather object.
Z ... Z ... Zephyr! A gentle breeze | one soft wind word to close the daily log.

[Interlude]
The forecast pauses—now call the weather words back.
